Room 2 · Timeline

The Earth in Our Hands

From the first buried seeds to today's supermarkets: follow the furrows that shaped the food on El Salvador's tables. Tap any era to expand.

World Territory Crops Food

World: Over 900,000 years ago, hunter-gatherer groups left Africa in search of daily sustenance, crafting stone, wood, and bone tools before settling down, raising livestock, and using pottery to store and cook food.

Crops: Thanks to its rich biodiversity, Mesoamerican domestication focused on plants and did not need draft animals. Farmers transformed wild teosinte into corn and a bitter wild gourd into squash (ayote), whose leaves retain soil moisture and whose spines deter pests.

Territory (2,000–1,400 BC): The first farming communities emerge alongside La Milpa (the Mesoamerican triad of corn, beans, and squash, grown with chili and tomato), worked collectively. Larger villages become barter hubs with tianguis (open-air markets) near ritual centers.

💡 Did you know? In the wild, only the oily seeds (pepitas) of the ayote were edible, and nomads used its hollow shell to gather fruit. Meanwhile, cacao (kacaw) was reserved exclusively for kings and nobles in sacred rituals.

Territory: Trade routes linked settlements such as Tazumal, San Andrés, Cihuatán, and Quelepa with Copán and Mexico. Two eruptions reshaped the land: Ilopango Volcano (431) and Laguna Caldera (630), which buried Joya de Cerén under six meters of ash.

Migrations (800–1200): Droughts, wars, and social collapse led to the abandonment of major Maya cities. Toltec and central/southern Mexican migrations followed, shaping the Señorío de Cuscatlán (Pipil people), where the cacique distributed communal land.

Food: Diets combined farming, hunting, and fishing: turkeys, ducks, jutes (river snails), coastal shellfish, salt-cured fish, edible flowers (loroco, izote, pito, squash blossoms), and zompopos (leafcutter ants). Food was stored in clay pots or underground pits called chultunes.

💡 Did you know? Vast yuca (cassava) plantations discovered at Joya de Cerén challenged the idea that the Maya relied solely on corn. In addition, cacao beans served as currency, and ritual chocolate was drunk cold, foamy, bitter, spiced with chili, and sometimes mixed with hallucinogenic mushrooms.

Territory: Following Columbus's landing (1492)—bringing horses, cattle, and donkeys in 1493—the Señorío de Cuscatlán fell in 1524. San Salvador was founded in 1525, abandoned under Indigenous resistance, and refounded in 1528 at today's Ciudad Vieja (La Bermuda, 10 km south of Suchitoto).

World & Crops: American crops like the tomato transformed European cuisine. In the Americas, colonizers introduced wheat, barley, mills, and plowed sugarcane monocultures, disrupting the diverse balance of la milpa.

Food: Culinary mestizaje was forged by Indigenous women cooking in Spanish homes and convents. Spaniards quickly traded metal cookware for local clay pots, while grinding stones, the comal, and tabancos (storage shelves above the hearth) endured.

💡 Did you know? At Ciudad Vieja, archaeologists uncovered a bread oven and a trash pit full of knife-cut bones where European species (pigs, cattle, chickens) sat right alongside Mesoamerican fauna (turkeys, dogs, frogs, and jutes).

Territory: Forced cash tributes (1540), genocide, slavery, and epidemics (1581–1585) decimated the native population. Ancestral communal land management was dismantled to make way for haciendas.

Crops (1600): The era of the "blue monoculture" arrives: from Santa Ana to San Miguel, the most fertile lands shifted from cacao to jiquilite to produce indigo (añil), sustained by the exploitation of peasants, mulattoes, and enslaved laborers in obrajes. Families tried to squeeze their milpas into the gaps between indigo harvests.

Food: Cattle replaced forests with pasture, and citrus, mangoes, and bananas arrived from Asia and Africa. Though initially hesitant to eat beef, Indigenous communities embraced dairy and cheese, while artisanal panela replaced honey as a sweetener.

💡 Did you know? European aristocrats began adding colonial sugar to fashionable drinks—tea, coffee, and chocolate—that were never sweetened in their places of origin. As Indigenous labor vanished in cacao fields, slave ships arrived in the Americas to work the sugarcane plantations.

Territory: In 1783, El Salvador became Central America's top indigo exporter—at the cost of irreversibly deforesting the northern hills. Independence (1821) kept Indigenous peoples excluded and sparked the Nonualco uprising (1832).

Crops (1840): Under the Coffee Republic, the last Indigenous communal lands were expropriated in the name of "progress," and vagrancy laws forced rural workers onto estates. Coffee became the economic engine, concentrating land in the hands of wealthy landowners.

Food: Elites equated fine dining with abundant meat; working-class tables relied on corn and beans seasoned with loroco, chipilín, and occasional dried meat or offal, while sugarcane and cattle displaced staple crops.

💡 Did you know? In 1700, Spain banned olive and grape cultivation in the Americas to protect its monopoly. Later, coffee shaped national life so deeply that school vacations were set in December and January so rural families could work the harvest (la corta).

Territory: Under the colonato system, landless peasants worked estates in exchange for food or shelter. Military dictatorships began in 1931, and in 1932 a popular uprising was met with the Indigenous Genocide, shattering cultural and ancestral ties to the land.

World: World War I (1914) popularized canned food and instant coffee from the trenches; by 1927, inspired by the Inuit, Clarence Birdseye launched the quick-frozen food industry.

Crops (1945): After World War II, chemical-weapons factories pivoted to pesticides and synthetic fertilizers, driving the Green Revolution (Rockefeller Foundation): it promised to end hunger, but degraded soils and entrenched monocultures.

🌱 Seeds at a Glance: Traditional: selected and shared by farmers for millennia; a guarantee of food sovereignty. Hybrid: unstable crossbreeds demanding more water, fertilizer, and constant repurchasing. GMO: lab-engineered to resist herbicides or make their own insecticide, eroding biodiversity and handing control to industry.

Territory: The Civil War (1981–1992) left thousands dead or disappeared, paralyzed farming, and pushed displaced rural families into the capital's poverty belts. The 1992 Peace Accords transferred land to former combatants, but the Land Bank closed in 1998.

World & Trade (1989–1990): The IMF and World Bank eliminated basic food price controls. The 1990 Free Trade Treaty let tariff-free, subsidized U.S. grains flood local markets, hurting national production, while returning farmers grew dependent on commercial seed-and-chemical packages.

Food: Processed foods entered via foreign aid, retail consolidated into two supermarket giants (Grupo Calleja and Walmart), and the spread of fast food fueled an obesity and diabetes crisis (denounced by the WHO since 1997).

✊ Resistance: As corporations like Monsanto patented GMO seeds—forbidding farmers from saving seeds to replant—grassroots movements emerged: Slow Food in Italy and La Vía Campesina (1993) defending agroecology and Food Sovereignty.

Today: Domestic production still cannot feed the country, leaving El Salvador reliant on imported industrial foods (mostly from Guatemala and the U.S.). Lack of nutritional education, low prices, and aggressive marketing push the lowest-income households toward ultra-processed foods. (Native corn varieties—negrito, amarillo, ulupilse, maravilla, and tizate—need fewer chemicals than white corn, yet are rarely commercialized.)

Soils: Artificial fertilizers lock up nutrients, deplete the earth, and pollute aquifers.

Crops: Vast monocultures grow fattening feed concentrates instead of local food.

Industrial Meat: Crowded livestock fed corn, synthetic estrogens, and heavy doses of antibiotics.

Sugars: Addictive corn syrup and nutrient-free refined sucrose, linked to obesity and diabetes.

Most Consumed: Sugar, instant soups, palm oil, processed meats, sodas, sweets, and packaged pastries.

Soils: Natural compost made from organic waste feeds soil bacteria and protects aquifers.

Crops: La milpa (corn, beans, chili, and squash) preserves soil fertility and ensures year-round food.

Ecological Livestock: Respectful raising on pastures and crop leftovers, reducing land dedicated to cattle.

Sugars: Natural fructose in fresh fruit and honey, plus artisanal chemical-free panela.

Most Eaten: Beans, corn tortillas, cheese, cream, bananas, plantains, tomatoes, onions, potatoes, chicken, and meat.
